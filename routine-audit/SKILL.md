# Routine Audit

When I ask you to run a routine audit, help me look at the work I repeat and
decide how much of it should be handed to AI. Not everything should be. The
point of this is to end up with an honest sort, not a longer list of automations.
Ask me what you need before you start, and do not assume.

## The three levels of autonomy

Every recurring task lands in one of these. Say which, and say why.

1. **Full autonomy.** It runs on a schedule or a trigger without me there, and I
   review the output rather than doing the work. Right when the task is
   repetitive, the inputs are predictable, and a mistake is cheap and easy to spot.
2. **Partial autonomy.** I am still in the loop. The AI drafts, checks,
   summarizes, or looks things up, and I decide. Right when the judgment is the
   job and the typing is not. Most work belongs here.
3. **Minimal or zero autonomy.** No AI, or so little it is not worth the setup.
   Right when the task is rare, when the value is in me having done it, or when
   being wrong is expensive and hard to notice.

## Least agency

For anything moving to full autonomy, design it with the least agency that still
does the job. Name all four:

- **The smallest model and effort level** that handles the task. The cheapest
  thing that works, not the most capable thing available.
- **The narrowest access.** The specific files, folders, or connected tools it
  needs. Not my whole workspace, and not every connector I happen to have.
- **The narrowest actions.** Read-only wherever that is enough.
- **The stopping condition.** What makes it halt and come find me.

If you cannot tell me what it should be denied, you have not designed it yet.

## What to do

1. **Get the real list.** Ask me what I actually repeat: daily, weekly, monthly,
   and the quarterly things I forget until they arrive. Work from my list, not
   from a generic list of office tasks. If I give you three items, work with
   three. Do not pad it.
2. **Ask four questions per task.** What sets it off, what it produces, who sees
   the result, and what happens if it comes out wrong. That last one decides
   almost everything, so do not skip it or answer it for me.
3. **Sort it, and commit.** Put each task at one of the three levels and give me
   the one-line reason. Do not hedge across two levels. If you genuinely cannot
   place it, say what you would need to know.
4. **Name the review gate.** For anything above minimal autonomy, say exactly who
   checks, what they are checking for, and what it happens before. "I read the
   draft before it sends" is a review gate. "Review regularly" is not.
5. **Say what should not go autonomous.** Call out anything I am likely to want to
   run unattended that you think should stay at partial or minimal, and say plainly
   why. This is the most useful part of the audit and the part people skip.
6. **Pick one first move.** Choose a single task to change this week, and say
   what changing it involves. One real change beats a table of intentions.
7. **Show me the audit. Do not build anything.** Produce the sort, the gates, and
   the first move. Setting it up is a separate decision I make after reading it.

## Rules

- Decide on the cost of being wrong, not on what is technically possible. Most
  things can be automated. Fewer things should be.
- When you are torn between full and partial autonomy, pick partial. Moving up a level later is easy. Discovering six weeks of quiet errors
  is not.
- Do not recommend full autonomy for a task I have not done by hand enough times to know
  its edge cases. If you suspect that is the situation, ask.
- A review gate that has no named person, no specific thing being checked, and no
  moment it happens before is not a review gate. Rewrite it until it has all three.
- Do not invent tasks, frequencies, or consequences I did not tell you. If the
  list is thin, ask me for more rather than filling it in.
- Set nothing up, connect nothing, and schedule nothing unless I ask separately.

## Quality check before you finish

- Every task I named appears exactly once, at exactly one level.
- Every level assignment has a reason, and the reason refers to what happens when
  the task goes wrong.
- Every task above minimal autonomy has a review gate naming a person, a check,
  and a moment, and every full-autonomy task names its least-agency design.
- At least one thing is called out as better left alone, or you have told me
  honestly that nothing qualified.
- There is one specific first move, not a list of them.
- Nothing was built, connected, or scheduled.

If I hand you a list with no sense of what any of it costs when it fails, stop
and ask. That is the question the whole audit turns on.
